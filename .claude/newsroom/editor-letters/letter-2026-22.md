# Editor's Letter — Week 22, 2026

**Published:** Fri 29 May 2026, 08:00 AEST
**Drafted:** Sunday 24 May 2026
**Length:** 407 / target 350–450
**Format:** B (position)

---

The worst thing that happens to a restaurant in winter is not the cold. It is the honesty.

Summer here is cooperative. The light flatters everything — the food, the view, the service, the guests. A terrace that is genuinely unremarkable becomes, in January, a beautiful place to eat. A wine list with one good bottle seems fine when the bay is doing its work in the background. Winter removes all of that. When the crowd goes, what is left is what the place actually is.

I am not being cruel about this. Some of my favourite meals on the Peninsula have been eaten on grey Tuesday afternoons in rooms that held maybe eight tables, all occupied by people who had come specifically because they wanted to be there. That specificity matters. It changes the atmosphere, the pacing, the conversation you have with whoever is cooking. The decision to remain open through the quiet months — and to mean it, not just to run a skeleton service for whoever wanders past — is itself a kind of editorial statement. It tells you something about how a place thinks.

The issue with coverage, though, is that we have historically reflected the season back to readers rather than ahead of it. By the time a long-read on winter cellar doors lands, the reader already knows it is cold outside. What they do not know — what they often cannot find — is which places have genuinely committed to this period and which are coasting on reputation until November comes to bail them out.

That is the question Peninsula Insider should be answering this winter, and it is the question Lucien and Iris have been turning over independently. Not "where to go in winter" — any publication can produce that list — but which places are at their actual best right now, and why, and what makes those places different from the ones that would rather you waited. One of those answers, it turns out, involves a kitchen that has quietly become more interesting since it stopped trying to be interesting to everyone.

There is a version of the Peninsula that only reveals itself when it is not performing for you. This week, we begin to look at it properly.

— Tyler Lockhart, Editor
